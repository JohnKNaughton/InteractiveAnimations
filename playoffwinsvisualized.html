Who. The NBA 75th Anniversary Team, announced in October 2021 — 76 players rather than 75, because a tie in the voting was left unbroken.

† The twenty-four additions. Everyone carrying a dagger was left off that team, and they are here for three different reasons. Most are the perennially argued omissions: Dwight Howard, Tracy McGrady, Chris Webber, Vince Carter, Alex English, Bernard King, Sidney Moncrief, Dennis Johnson, Bob Lanier, Manu Ginóbili, Tony Parker, Pau Gasol, Ben Wallace, Draymond Green, Chauncey Billups and Grant Hill. Four built their case largely after the 2021 voting closed — Nikola Jokić, Jayson Tatum, Luka Dončić and Joel Embiid. The rest are here because a chart about playoff series is incomplete without them: Robert Horry and Tom Heinsohn, who between them own fifteen championships, plus Klay Thompson and Jimmy Butler.

Who belongs on such a list is an argument, not a fact. The dagger keeps the official team recoverable, and the roster control drops all twenty-four if you want the seventy-six exactly as voted.

What counts. A playoff series won by the team a player was on that season. Seasons are labelled by the year the playoffs were played, so 1996–97 appears as 1997. Nothing here is entered per player: each player's stack for a season is read off that season's bracket via the team he finished the year with, so two teammates can never disagree about a season.

Credit follows the roster, not the minutes. A player on a title team gets the run whether he starred in it, came off the bench, or watched it injured — so Patrick Ewing keeps New York's 1999 Finals run and Elgin Baylor's 1972 title, won after he retired nine games into the season, is not counted, because he was not on the playoff roster. Mid-season trades are credited to the team a player finished on. ABA seasons are excluded; the league's own record books do the same.

Why the towers are shorter before 1984. The bracket has not always been four rounds. Through 1983 the best teams often had first-round byes, so a title could be won in two or three series — Minneapolis won the 1954 championship in two. The orange cap marks the title regardless of how many rounds it took, and the faint rule at 1984 in each row marks where the format settled.
